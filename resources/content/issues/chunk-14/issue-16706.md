# An external plane cannot recover itself: it breaks, we see it, and nothing brings it back

**Converted from a hand-maintained tracker to an Epic on 2026-08-09.** The prior body carried a hardcoded MUST/SHOULD sub-list and an ownership table — the exact shape `epic-create` names as an automatic failure, because it goes stale the moment a sub is added, closed or re-scoped. It had been hand-refreshed three times in one day and was still wrong an hour later. The board and the operator runbook are preserved verbatim as comments; the live sub-set is the parent-child graph on this issue, never prose here.

## Problem scope

A deployed Agent OS plane can break and stay broken, and every layer that should have caught it reports normally.

The grounding observation, measured on an external plane on 2026-08-08 and unchanged on 2026-08-09:

- its Knowledge Base has **never ingested** — `count: 0`, all tenant repos backoff-suppressed at the cadence cap;
- its model container holds **~4 cores sustained for 24+ hours** against a vector store holding **12 MB of 8 GB** — re-measured 2026-08-10 as **97 % of a 48-hour uptime**, with the KB container at **0 % CPU** and a corpus of **0**, i.e. burning with nothing asking it to;
- its Memory Core sits **`unhealthy` with its process alive** — serving, and marked dead;
- the host itself is idle: 64 cores at 7.4 % load, 252 GB at 10 %.

**Four lanes at full tilt for a day, and nothing in the store.** That is not "too slow to finish"; it is work that is not landing, on a machine with enormous headroom, invisible to every diagnostic surface we ship.

### Why this needs an Epic rather than one ticket

The failure is not one defect. It is one *outcome* — a plane that cannot recover itself — reached independently by several subsystems: the ingestion path, the health-diagnosis path, the actuation path, the ledger that should have recorded it, and the ceilings that bound it. Each has a different owner and a different fix, and **no single PR delivers the outcome**. Coordination across those lanes, with a shared definition of done, is the whole content.

### The definition of stable this Epic is measured against

> **"It broke, we saw it, and it came back on its own."**

Three clauses. They are not equally hard and they were not equally missing:

| clause | state at Epic creation |
|---|---|
| **it broke** — fewer ways to break | shipped |
| **we saw it** — the failure is visible | shipped |
| **it came back on its own** | **did not exist** |

The third clause is why this is an Epic and not a closed checklist. Detection and observability both landed while recoverability remained zero — a plane could diagnose itself perfectly and still require a human.

## Intended solution shape

**Close the loop, without granting the loop new authority.**

The organism already had every half it needed and no connection between them: an actuator that was shipped, admission-gated and anti-thrash-enveloped; a diagnosis layer emitting `critical`, `authoritative` facts; a durable ledger. What was missing was the *edge* — and, one layer up, the second evidence channel the governing ADR requires before that edge may act.

The convergent shape the subs serve:

1. **Evidence before action.** A restart is licensed by two independent channels — the runtime's verdict **and** a direct answer from the service itself — never by one channel evaluated repeatedly. Debouncing answers noise; it cannot answer contradiction. A service that is answering is never restarted, whatever its healthcheck says.
2. **Consume the decision.** A diagnosis that reaches no actuator is indistinguishable from a healthy system to everything except a human reading a dashboard.
3. **Bound what acts.** Anti-thrash, per-effect authority, and a closed action set that implementation work may not widen.
4. **Record where it can be read.** A heal-event written to a path no consumer reads is not observability.
5. **Bound what the plane spends.** Provider consumption must be attributable to, and bounded by, work in flight — an empty corpus must not be able to sustain four cores.

**What this Epic explicitly does not attempt:** ~~making a starved provider fast. The empty corpus is caused on the plane's own side.~~ Nothing here produces rows. What the work does is make the failure *survivable, visible and recoverable* — the difference between "it broke again" and the three-clause definition above.

## Out of scope

- **The external plane's own decisions** — provider capacity, embedding parallelism, endpoint isolation, who runs its rebuild, and its `offHostSyncConfigured: false` durability posture. None of these are Neo tickets and no sub can close them.
- **Re-parenting independently-justified lanes.** Several tickets overlap this outcome and pre-date it — the Docker Agent OS hard-cut, the `ai/deploy` layering, the Chroma persist-path work, the heap-ceiling measurement, the Memory Core wedge. They are `Related`, not subs: they were not created to serve this outcome, and claiming them would drag six maintainers' independent lanes into one closeout.
- **Declaring any lane complete here.** A sub closes on its own ticket; this Epic never leads that.
- The client's identity, hostnames, credentials or business context.

## Avoided traps

- **Reading a full checklist as a working corpus.** Every MUST item can close while the plane still ingests nothing. The tiers gate *deployment stability*, never *ingestion success* — and conflating them is the single most likely misreading of this Epic.
- **Assuming detection implies recovery.** This is the empirical one: two of three clauses shipped, and recoverability stayed at exactly zero. A new build genuinely improved diagnosis and changed recoverability by nothing at all.
- **Restarting on one evidence channel.** The governing ADR names the live instance: a provider-dependent canary false-fails while the service still answers and persists, so restarting it is a self-inflicted outage. An implementation that "let one authoritative fact classify" was written, reviewed, falsified and reverted inside this Epic — the retraction is on its sub, because the wrong version was published.
- **Asserting a deployment property from a checked-in file.** Repeatedly committed here, in both directions: a compose layer was grepped and a property of a *running machine* was claimed. What we can offer an operator we cannot reach is a **verifiable precondition**, never an assertion.
- **Ordering config after code.** The intuitive order — get current code on, then fix config — is the one that strands a plane whose start-gates were never re-evaluated. `depends_on` conditions bind at start, so "it is up right now" and "it will come back" are different properties, and only the first is observable from outside.
- **A hand-maintained sub-list.** Demonstrated by this issue's own first 42 KB.

## Root-cause refinement — the provider is a bounded worker on an idle host, not an exhausted one

Host telemetry from the external plane, 2026-08-09T01:26:19Z. This sharpens the *"Not Neo tickets"* section's first entry, which currently reads **"embedding provider capacity (the actual root cause)"**.

### The host has enormous headroom

```
64-core AMD EPYC 7502P          load  4.42 / 4.42 / 4.53   →  7.1%
252 G RAM                       used 24.7 G (9.8%) · free 27.3 G · cached 193 G
swap 4.00 G                     used 13.0 M (0.3%)
```

Roughly sixty cores are idle while the provider container runs at ~400% CPU. *(See the correction above: that ~400% is four active embedding lanes, not a quota.)*

### The decisive number is the pairing, not the percentage

```
ollama runner   CPU-time 62:57:35   uptime 17 h   →  3.70 cores sustained, continuously
chroma          12.0 M / 8.00 G resident
```

**3.7 cores have run flat out for seventeen hours and the store is essentially empty.** Sustained full-tilt compute with no corpus growth is not "too slow to finish" — it is work that is not landing. Those are different failures:

| reading | implied fix |
|---|---|
| provider too slow for the workload | more/faster hardware, or a smaller model |
| ~~provider pinned at a configured ceiling while the host idles~~ | ~~a parallelism/thread setting~~ — **retracted, see correction** |
| **provider busy but producing nothing durable** | the failure is downstream of compute — the result never reaches the store |

The third is the one the evidence supports. **A strong candidate now exists for the mechanism: `#14154` — *"KB-sync embedder 404s mid-sync ('resource could not be found' / model not resident) — root-cause the eviction."*** If the embedding model is evicted to serve the chat model, embed calls fail while the runner stays busy — four lanes, 404s, no rows. That ticket is filed and idle; checking it costs nothing before anyone deploys.

**Still not a Neo ticket.** The refinement is only that *"nothing below makes a starved provider fast"* may answer the wrong question if the provider is busy rather than slow.

### Second, and this one IS ours

The same readout shows the Memory Core container **`unhealthy` with its process alive** — 710 M resident, 0.9% CPU — while the Knowledge Base container beside it is `healthy`. That is `#16677`'s signature, live, and it reproduces that ticket's original control exactly.

That eliminates missing-policy, missing-privilege and missing-actuator, leaving the absent controller.

> ### 📐 READ THE 400 % CORRECTLY — added 2026-08-10 08:2xZ, and it changes which lever you reach for
>
> **`~400 %` is almost certainly your container's CPU *cap*, not a measure of how much work is happening.** The shipped model service is capped at:
>
> ```yaml
> cpus:   "${OLLAMA_CPU_LIMIT:-4.0}"
> memory: "${OLLAMA_MEMORY_LIMIT:-48g}"
> ```
>
> `4.0` CPUs is exactly `400 %` in `docker stats` / `glances`. **So "400 %" means "pinned at its ceiling" and tells you nothing about whether the work is real.** Two very different states produce the identical number, and the discriminator is *duration*, not magnitude:
>
> - **legitimately busy** — a runner at the cap that returns to ~0 % between requests;
> - **stuck** — a runner at the cap **continuously**. Re-measure with `TIME+` from `top`/`glances` rather than the instantaneous percentage: divide accumulated CPU-time by the percentage to get wall-time, and compare that against container uptime. On the observed plane this was **185:56:12 of CPU time at ~398 %, i.e. 46.7 wall-hours out of a ~48-hour uptime — pegged for 97 % of its life**, while the KB container sat at **0 % CPU** with an **empty** corpus. Nothing was asking it to work, and it never stopped.
>
> **The second thing the cap tells you, and it is the actionable one: your embeddings are slow by CONFIGURATION, not by hardware.** A 64-thread host running at 7 % is giving the model **four** of those threads — and with `OLLAMA_MAX_LOADED_MODELS=2` plus `OLLAMA_KEEP_ALIVE=-1`, a chat model and an embedding model are both resident and **competing for the same four**.
>
> **That single fact drives the timeout problem underneath everything else.** Every deadline in Neo's embedding path is sized for a model that answers in seconds. On four shared CPU threads a cold embed can take tens of seconds — and **an embedding request that is abandoned mid-flight is what strands the runner in the first place** (`#16853`). So the cap does not merely make things slow; **it manufactures the aborts that cause the wedge, and the wedge then holds the cap at 100 % forever.**
>
> **Three levers, in the order that gives the most relief per unit of risk:**
>
> | lever | what to do | why it is first/second/third |
> |---|---|---|
> | **1. Raise the CPU cap** | `OLLAMA_CPU_LIMIT` from `4.0` to `16.0`–`32.0` on a 64-thread host, then recreate the model service | Attacks the cause rather than the symptom. Shortens every embed, so the deadlines below stop firing at all. Your host is at **7 %** — this capacity is already paid for and currently unused. |
> | **2. Raise the embedding deadline** | `NEO_OLLAMA_EMBEDDING_TIMEOUT_MS` (default `300000`) | Headroom while the cap change takes effect. **Note it only became settable on a containerized plane very recently** — before that fix it could not reach the container at all, so if you tried to set it previously and saw no effect, that is why. |
> | **3. Shrink the work unit** | `NEO_KB_EMBEDDING_BATCH_SIZE` (default `50`) down to `5`–`10` | A smaller batch is a shorter request, which is less likely to cross any deadline. Same remedy as (2) from the other direction. |
>
> **Do not reach for (2) or (3) alone if (1) is available.** Raising a timeout makes a slow system wait longer; raising the cap makes it stop being slow. And **verify the parallelism knobs are actually applied** — the profile pins `OLLAMA_NUM_PARALLEL=1`, but a deployment running an un-recreated container can be on ollama's auto-default instead, which multiplies both threads and KV cache:
>
> ```
> docker exec <model-container> env | grep -E 'OLLAMA_NUM_PARALLEL|OLLAMA_MAX_LOADED_MODELS|OLLAMA_CONTEXT_LENGTH'
> ```
>

### Step 3.6 — 🎯 GET ONE BATCH THROUGH *(added 2026-08-10 00:0xZ — if your corpus has never ingested, this is the whole morning)*

**Do not try to make the Knowledge Base "work". Try to land one batch.** Everything else follows from that, and the reason is proved in Step 4: our ingest path persists incrementally, so **one successful batch takes your count from `0` to non-zero permanently and it can never go back.** A two-month standstill and a working corpus are separated by a single batch.

**Why it has not landed, mechanically.** The unit that must succeed is larger than it looks. `batchSize` defaults to **50 chunks**, and the whole slice is embedded in one provider call before *anything* is written — so all 50 land together or none do. Underneath, that is ten provider requests (`batchEmbeddingChunkSize`, default 5), and **any one of them exhausting its retries discards all 50.** On a provider that is merely slow, a big enough bet never wins.

**So shrink the bet until it wins.** Set these on the services that ingest, then run one sync:

```

# give a slow model room — raise the one matching YOUR provider (Step 0.5 told you which)
NEO_OPENAI_COMPATIBLE_BATCH_EMBEDDING_TIMEOUT_MS=900000
NEO_OLLAMA_EMBEDDING_TIMEOUT_MS=900000
```

**And if your image contains `PR #16847`, the decisive one — shrink the durable unit itself:**

```
NEO_KB_EMBEDDING_BATCH_SIZE=1      # one chunk is now the whole bet
NEO_KB_EMBEDDING_BATCH_DELAY_MS=0  # do NOT skip this: the default waits 10s BETWEEN batches
NEO_KB_EMBEDDING_MAX_RETRIES=2     # optional: fail faster while you are still experimenting
```

> **`NEO_KB_EMBEDDING_BATCH_DELAY_MS=0` is not optional if you set batch size to 1.** That delay is paid *between* batches, so a 50× smaller batch means 50× more waits.
